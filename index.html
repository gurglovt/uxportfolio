Note-taking app: Dumpy

A note-taking app built for the frantic moment an idea strikes.

Problem

Current note-taking apps create friction at exactly the wrong moment. To capture a fleeting idea you need to open the app, locate or create a note, and start typing — all while the idea is already fading. The result: fumbling, frustration, and a graveyard of disorganised notes where good ideas go to be buried.

Solution

Dumpy treats rapid capture as the primary use case. Its core feature — the 'Dump' — works like a text feed you can scrawl into before the app is even fully open. A clean hierarchical notebook system then gives every scrawl a home when there's time to organise.

Process

  1. Empathise — user interviews, personas, competitive audit
  2. Define — feature list & project scope
  3. Initial wireframes — paper sketches & user feedback
  4. Low-fidelity prototype — build & test
  5. High-fidelity prototype — build & test
  6. Finalise — further iteration & AI-assisted prototyping

01 Empathise

User interviews

4 interviews with regular phone users. Key findings:

  • Apps weren't cutting it. Pre-installed apps (iPhone Notes, Google Keep, Samsung Notes) consistently fell short. Users often reached for whatever had least friction — chat apps like Teams, Discord, or ChatGPT.
  • Every idea = a new note. Users accumulated junk notes with no structure; one called it "messy and inconvenient".
  • Demand for a scratchpad. Users wanted a 'whiteboard' — somewhere to quickly dump and delete shifting information.
  • Notifications were well-used across Keep and Calendar, but required constant maintenance.

Competitive audit

Audited Google Keep, Evernote, and Notion. Highlights:

  • Notion's block system is impressively flexible — hierarchical pages, sub-pages, and paragraph-level editing.
  • Keep & Evernote's tile navigation shows note previews but makes higher-level structure hard to visualise.
  • Evernote's Scratchpad was the closest thing to the whiteboard feature users wanted.
  • Free folder/notebook grouping was largely paywalled across competitors.

02 Define

Responding to research, I mind-mapped user problems against proposed solutions in FigJam to define scope.

Hover to expand the image.

Initial feature list

  • Scratchpad / 'Dump': The headline feature. Modelled on messenger apps for speed — minimal friction to capture, with the option to pipe content into a saved note. Core USP.
  • Hierarchical file structure: Notes live in Notebooks; sub-notes nest under notes.
  • Notion-style blocks: Paragraph-level formatting and tokenisation.
  • Block-level notifications: Attach a reminder to any block of text.
  • Lists section: A dedicated space for grocery/chore-style lists with reminder integration.

Test 1 — Feedback on initial wireframes

Showed screenshots to 2 users. Goals: could they understand the app; where was it confusing?

Findings

  • Lists appearing in both Notebooks and a separate Lists section was confusing.
  • Similar-looking elements doing different things in different places caused disorientation.
  • The 'Dump' was opaque — users didn't understand what it was, what it did, or why it had that name.
Test script (click to expand)

Preface by saying that these are screenshots and aren't interactable.

For each of the four screens, ask:

  1. Could you tell me what this is?
  2. Could you tell me what you think you can do here?

And finally:

  1. What are your overall thoughts when you look at the four screens together?

Responses & actions

  • Make the Dump self-explanatory. New users must grasp it instantly — it's the app's core differentiator.
  • Consolidate Lists into Notebooks. Since lists-within-notes was already planned, a separate Lists section only adds confusion. Aim to remove the bottom navigation entirely.
  • Reduce button count and visual similarity. Too many arrow-style buttons; rework the structure to need fewer.

Paper iterations

What changed

  1. 4 sections → 3. Notebooks, Home, Dump only. (Quickly abandoned — too much emphasis on notebooks.)
  2. Dump at thumb level. Move emphasis to the Dump; put Notebooks in a drawer. (Drawer felt unintuitive.)

  4. Final paper concept: Dump at the bottom, file navigation above, with adjustable split height. ✓ Chosen for the lo-fi prototype.

04 Low-fidelity prototype

What this prototype tested

  • The Dump — usage and integration into the app
  • Text tools within the Dump
  • Overall layout, file navigation, information hierarchy

Design decisions

  • Home screen (top → bottom): Search bar → horizontal notebook scroll → active notebook with file tree (including checkbox list items) → Dump section. The Dump can collapse or expand to fullscreen.
  • Dump fullscreen reveals the text editor. Same editor used for normal notes — toolbar above the keyboard edits the active block.
  • Large parts left unbuilt intentionally — faster to ship and test the key interactions.
  • A 🏠 button served as a prototype-only "restart" workaround for Figma's lack of swipe-back support.

3 users, think-aloud protocol. Testing comprehension, expected vs actual button behaviour, and friction points.

Test script (click to expand)
  1. Ask the user what phone they use and how much time they spend using it each day.
  2. Warn the user that this is not a fully-functioning app: Tell them that many of the buttons don't do anything. Let them know that most of the graphical elements of the design haven't been added yet, and that we are just testing the overall functionality of the app. Let them know that later in the test, a "home" button will appear in the corner if they ever get stuck and want to return to the start, but this is a feature of the test and not the app.
  3. Warn the user that you will be reading from a script to maintain consistency across tests with other users.
  4. Tell the user to think out loud throughout the test, and particularly to point out anything that feels unintuitive or unexpected to them, at any time.
  5. Ask the user to look at the home screen and give me their first impressions on what the app is and does, from top to bottom.

Home screen tasks:

  1. What is the list in the middle showing? What can you infer from how it's structured?
  2. How would you see notes from the 'Journal' notebook?
  3. How do you feel about the layout here [in the 'Journal' section]?
  4. What kinds of notebooks/how many would you keep if you were using this app for yourself?
  5. What do you think the 'Dump' is? [If they haven't said anything already]
  6. How would you add content to your Dump?
  7. Before you do that, what do you think the other buttons around the Dump do?
  8. How do you feel about the Dump right now?

Dump tasks:

  1. First, where are you now? [In the Dump section]
  2. What are your first impressions of what you see?
  3. Do you see the bar above your keyboard? What do you think it does?
  4. Go ahead and try out some of the buttons on the toolbar. Your changes won't be able to be applied simultaneously, so you'll have to undo your changes to try other buttons. Make sure to tell me what you're thinking before and after you press each button.
  5. Would you make use of the Dump if it was a feature in your favourite note-taking app?
  6. On your own phone, how would you go back to the home screen (without using the home button)?

Questions after tasks:

  1. What do you think of what you've seen today? Do you have any broad opinions about the app right now?
  2. Supposing that this app was in a finished condition, do you think that you would use it over your current note-taking method?

Actions for the hi-fi

  • Fill placeholders — make it look like a finished product so users don't have to imagine.
  • Add dark mode — one user called it a dealbreaker.
  • Adjustable Dump height — a drag handle replaces multiple confusing buttons.
  • Visually distinguish the Dump — make clear it's separate from the notebook hierarchy.
  • Add menu options — settings, note/notebook options, so the app feels complete.
  • Rework toolbar icons — several were confusing or had unclear hit zones.
  • Highlight notified blocks clearly — but not distractingly.
  • Add text search in editor — simple request, easy to add at this stage.

Test script (click to expand)
  1. Let them know that you will read from a script for consistency across tests.
  2. Welcome to the test! I'll first ask you to keep in mind that I'm not testing you. Rather, today you'll be testing a prototype of an app, and we'll be checking to see how easy the app is to understand and use. By "prototype", we mean that this is not a real, fully-developed app, but instead a blueprint of how the final product should look and feel. Many of the buttons don't have any functionality assigned to them, but you will still be able to do everything we hope to, provided that the app is easy enough to use.
  3. Before we get started, I'd like to thank you for participating and let you know that it really helps us to know how to develop the product. In order for us to get the most out of the session today, I'd like to urge you to think out loud as much as possible. Any time something is unintuitive, or isn't where you expect it to be, or is frustrating, please let me know. Don't worry about hurting anyone's feelings – we really do want to hear all your reactions, complaints and all.
  4. Let's get started. Please open the app and, before you tap anything, let me know your initial reactions to what you think the app broadly is, and what you think it can do.
    1. What sticks out to you?
    2. Is there anything that has an unclear function?
  5. Let's look at the 'Dump' on the lower half of the screen. Let me know your reactions to it if you haven't already.
  6. Your task now is to find out what the 'Dump' is, and what it can do. Remember to think and react out loud, and let me know if anything is frustrating or unintuitive.
  7. Tell the user to finish exploring for now. Could you summarise your reactions to the 'Dump'?
    1. Could you figure out what it was/did?
    2. Would you use this feature if it was in your favourite note-taking app?
  8. Restart the prototype, contract the Dump. Your next task is to figure out how this navigation system works. Remember to think and react out loud, and let me know if anything is frustrating or unintuitive.
  9. Tell the user to finish exploring for now. What did you learn?
    1. How are notes in this app organised?
    2. Does this system make sense to you?
    3. How does this compare to the note organisation system on your favourite note-taking app?
  10. Your next task is to add some text to a note or to the 'Dump'. You can do this in the 'Biscuit Recipe' note, or in the full-screen 'Dump'.
  11. How does this text editor view compare to your expectations for a note-taking app?
  12. Finally, please go through the icons on the toolbar, and let me know what you expect each one to do. You can test these buttons too, but bear in mind that, due to the limitations of the prototype, only one button can be active at a time. Please undo your changes after you apply them in order to apply a different change. Again, remember to state your predictions, and to think and react out loud.
  13. That concludes the tasks for this test! Finally, I'll just ask a few questions about you and your experience today.
    1. What phone do you use?
    2. What is your favourite note-taking app?
    3. Did the term "notebook" make sense to you? How would you explain what a "notebook" is in this app?
    4. Did the term "pipe" make sense to you? How would you explain what "piping [content]" is in this app?
    5. Would you use this app over your favourite note-taking app? Why/Why not? What key features are present/absent?
    6. This app is called "Dumpy". What are your reactions to that name?
  14. Thank you very much for all of your help today! Have a wonderful day!

Key findings

  • Praise for the Dump — users liked the concept and grasped it quickly. The navigation system was well-received.
  • Toolbar icons were confusing — block up/down buttons unclear; serif toggle not intuitive. Missing: undo, bullet points.
  • Visual hierarchy too dense — headings too small; overall layout felt tight. Fonts too small.
  • The Dump's relationship to notes was still unclear — some users weren't sure if it was part of the notebook or separate.
  • "Pipe" didn't land — users preferred "Move". The name "Dumpy" got a mixed but curious reception.

06 Finalise

Further wireframing

Taking stock of test 3's feedback, I explored new iterations focused on making the layout less overwhelming, separating the Dump more clearly, and giving everything more room to breathe.

Click to expand.

What changed

  • Dump as a floating bubble — tapping it expands it and darkens the background, clearly separating "note view mode" from "Dump mode".
  • Notebook tabs as pills — replacing the icon grid with a horizontal pill row. More scannable, less visual noise.
  • Notes and Dump extended edge-to-edge — removing the tight card borders that made the layout feel cramped.
  • Reduced thin lines — fewer dividers throughout; more whitespace, more playful tone.
  • "Pipe" → "Move" — responding directly to user feedback.
  • Tapping a note title edits it in place; pencil icon replaces the old "edit name" menu item.

New toolbar icons & fonts

I also redesigned all the toolbar icons, and chose new typefaces. Both changes improve readability, but the playful serif (Crimson Pro) on note titles and the neater sans-serif (Rubik) for body text also increase the app's sense of warmth and playfulness.

Local creative discovery: CrowdFinder

"Find your crowd." A platform for local creatives to share work, discover each other, and build real communities.

Problem

Creative isolation is a persistent problem that mainstream social media makes worse. Sharing work publicly gamifies it, exposes it to the wrong audiences, and turns creators into isolated public figures — all while remaining in their own geographic bubbles. Online communities are great, but art and creativity have a tactility that is best shared in person. Social media platforms tend to neglect to promote offline socialisation.

Solution

CrowdFinder brings locality to content-sharing, and discoverability to local people-finding. It's an ethical, non-profit-oriented platform combining portfolio hosting, a proximity-aware discovery feed, and direct messaging — with no visible like counts, no paywalled discoverability, and optional pseudonymity.

Process

  1. Empathise — conceptualisation, competitive audit, personas, user problems & HMWs, user flow & happy paths

[Mockup coming soon]

01 Empathise

Conceptualisation

The internet is littered with secret art finstas, anonymous X poets, and off-grid SoundCloud musicians. It was apparent to me that many creatives simply don't know where to put their work, and in my own experience, casting your work into the open internet like that tends to be demoralising. There are doubtless hundreds of thousands of underappreciated creators not reaching an audience and not really getting what they wanted out of sharing their work. The obvious solution seemed to be to narrow the audience, and to do so in a way that promoted real, meaningful discussions and connections.

Social media tends to gamify engagement such that the author becomes a dehumanised content dispenser, and the engagement of the few who stumble across the work determine its worth. This causes creators to create with engagement in mind, undermining their intentions or their message to stay afloat in the attention economy game. This ultimately results in a less ethical, less fruitful and less enjoyable user experience for creators.

Competitive landscape

CrowdFinder combines content-sharing platforms and local people-finding platforms to create an ethical solution to the creator sharing problem. Neither category adequately addresses the other's territory. Content platforms (Instagram, Behance, Wattpad) ignore geography; local-discovery platforms (Meetup, Bumble) ignore real expression in favour of extreme practicality or irony-soaked self-consciousness.

Creative platforms like pixiv and YouTube can host incredibly interesting passion projects and works, but ultimately don't do much for creating communities, and have engagement-led algorithms which can leave really great work buried beneath the stuff that's more shareable. Changing this is key to improving the user experience of online creators and for making the act of sharing one's work more meaningful and beneficial, for creators and their communities.

Personas

I created 3 personas to capture a few different potential markets: John represents the self-conscious new creator, dipping his toe into a new medium and wanting feedback, and encouragement to keep going. Dalia is, as far as her social media is concerned, a successful artist, but she still feels isolated, and needs a platform that will help her to rebuild the community that was so important for her practice when she was a student. The East Liverpool Co. are an art collective. Art collectives can only form from artists that already know each other -- you can't post a job ad for a creative mind with some spare time. I think that CrowdFinder is serendipitously set up to facilitate recruitment for formal collectives, as well as for helping to create looser local art communities.

User problems

I laid out the main user problems below, and grouped them: 'The social media game' is about the impoverished social experience of gamifying social media; 'The creative sharing problem' is about finding a way, as an artist, to find an appropriate audience for work that can sometimes appeal only to very niche audiences; 'Community-building' is all about the ways in which users may want or benefit from growing their network, making new friends, or joining a community of like-minded individuals.

How might we…

Here I reframes the problems above into design opportunities. The six HMWs below guided ideation — covering community-building, sharing anxiety, rethinking engagement mechanics, discoverability, collective scouting, and exposure to challenging work.

User flow

The app has two major objectives: to make work discoverable, and to facilitate connections between users. For that reason, the app has three primary sections — the 'Portfolio' and 'Discover' sections for sharing your own work and discovering others', and the Messenger, for connection. In the discovery view, users may skim-browse many local portfolios at once, or be presented with a 'Discovery Queue' that allows them to consider one portfolio showcase at a time. This was added to encourage less scattered consideration for users one at a time, and to elevate users who had received less engagement.

Users should be able to go between these 3 sections with ease, and I've also shown how discovering an interesting portfolio leads quite frictionlessly to a chat window with the artist.

After signing up, we land on the portfolio view so that users can either appreciate their work before moving on to discover other users, or to subtly prompt them to upload some work to share.

Happy paths

Three core happy paths were mapped: onboarding a new user, finding and messaging a local creator through the discovery queue, and adding new content to a portfolio.
