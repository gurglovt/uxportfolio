Notetaking app: Dumpy

November 2025 - March 2026
(part-time)

Dumpy is a personal note-taking app project.

Problem:

Capturing a fleeting idea can be frantic.

  • If you're in the middle of something when a great idea hits, you're in a bind. A note-taking app that's too finicky or unreliable can mean that your idea escapes the record.
  • The current array of note-taking apps all force unnecessary roadblocks on the user: to capture an idea, users have to open the app, then open a note or make a new one. Then they may have to tap a few times, make a new line, precisely hit a lot of tiny boxes even though time is of the essence.
  • This leads to fumbling and frustration when capturing the idea, and to a woefully disorganised note space after the fact.
  • Users collect an incomprehensible mass of ideas, here and there, from now and whenever, organised only by recency or name. This means that the more ideas you have, the more ideas you bury, and the the initial capture is rendered redundant.

Solution:

  • Dumpy is a note-taking app that accommodates the frantic user. It's built to be able to capture fleeting ideas with just a few lurches at the phone screen, while also arming them with the tools to precisely organise their hasty scrawls when they have a little more time.
  • It uses the 'Dump', a powerful whiteboard-like feature, to enable users to rapidly record an idea with the efficiency of sending a text, and before the user even opens the app.
  • On top of that, it uses an orderly, hierarchical file-structuring system to make sure that users can nest their ideas wherever it makes sense to them -- no more burying good ideas in a sea of new ideas.

Process (contents of this case study):

  1. Empathise: Conduct user interviews, create personas, conduct a competitive audit.
  2. Define: Respond to research and create a feature list; Define project scope.
  3. Initial wireframes: Create a set of initial wireframes and gather feedback from users.
  4. Low-fidelity prototype: Ideate on intial design based on user feedback, and create and test a low-fidelity prototype.
  5. High-fidelity prototype: Ideate on feedback from the test of the low-fidelity prototype, and create and test a high-fidelity prototype.

1. Empathise

User interviews:

To start the project, I conducted 4 interviews with regular people to see how they take notes, and what role note-taking apps play in the ideosyncratic memory-capture systems that they have each developed.

Findings:

  • Popular/pre-installed note-taking apps (iPhone Notes, Google Keep, Samsung Notes) were routinely not fulfilling the needs of its users.
  • Users tend to reach for whatever has the least amount of friction to take notes on -- users will sometimes use a chat with themselves on Microsoft Teams, Discord, or ChatGPT if it is the thing that is most convenient in the moment.
  • Interviewees found themselves making a whole new note for every new idea, and accumulating a lot of junk notes over time. One interviewee said that this was "messy and inconvenient".
  • Maintaining notes across Google Keep and Google Calendar required routine maintenance if it was to remain a useful place to see plans and ideas.
  • There seemed to be demand for a "whiteboard" feature, on the apps that are convenient, that would allow users to very quickly add, edit and delete information as it changes.
  • Interviewees made good use of notifications on notes and their calendar.

Competitive Audit:

I conducted a competitive audit across three competitor note-taking apps: Google Keep, Evernote, and Notion

Findings:

  • Competitors have a number of impressive features, for example Notion's hierarchical system of text "blocks", as well as "pages" and subordinate "sub-pages" allowed users to easily distinguish and edit entire paragraphs, and nest notes beneath related notes.
  • Notion also has a simple but powerful customisation system, where users can set the header and (emoji) icon of their note.
  • Evernote and Google Keep had a confusing tile-based file navigation system, which made it easy to see what was in a note, but made it difficult to navigate and manage notes or visualise the higher structure of notes.
  • Other apps didn't offer a folder system to group notes together without paying for premium versions.
  • Evernote's "Scratchpad" served the use of the 'whiteboard'-type feature that my interviewees from this phase seemed to demand.

2. Define

Defining scope is crucial for ensuring that a project can have positive and complete outcomes.

Initial feature list:

  • Evernote-style scratchpad system: Users need a way to quickly make notes, and I can make this immitate the situations where users quickly reach for Teams or Discord to make notes by making it resemble a messenger app.
    • Messenger apps are incredibly finely-tuned to be able to imitate the speed and ease of normal conversation, so imitating that design would help ease of use.
    • Users should be able to pipe what they put on their scratchpad/whiteboard into their notes.
    • I believe that this is the best way to enable users to make notes as quickly as possible, so have focused on it as a USP.
  • Clear, hierarchical file structure: Users can keep notes in "notebooks", which act as folders, and nest "subnotes" beneath "notes".
  • Notion-style "block" system: Notion's "block" system allowed for impressive speed when formatting text and tokenising paragraphs.
  • Ability to put a notification on any block: Users made good use of notifications, so implementing a system to notify them of notes that they've created would be very useful.
  • Lists section: Creating a separate "Lists" section seemed, at the time, to be a good way to increase the speed at which users could make lists.

Gathering user feedback - Test 1

Goals:

I presented these screenshots to 2 users to gather their responses and feedback. I wanted to:

  • find out if the function of the app was easy to work out.
  • find out if the structure of the app made sense.
  • collect points of confusion to prioritise for improvement.

Methodology:

  1. Show one of the four screenshots.
  2. Ask:
    1. Could you tell me what this is?
    2. Could you tell me what you think you can do here?
  3. Move onto the next screenshot and repeat.
  4. When we have looked at every screenshot, ask:
    1. What are your overall thoughts when you look at the four screens together?

Findings:

  • Lists being present in both the 'Notebooks' and 'Lists' section was confusing.
  • There was a broader issue of things looking the same and doing the same thing but being in different places causing confusion.
  • Some concerns raised about a lack of consolidation within the app, and a lack of clarity about how the different sections interact.
  • Confusion around the 'Dump' section: Users didn't understand what it was, what it did, or why it was called 'Dump'.

Responses and action:

  • Make the 'Dump' clear: New users should be taken more into account when presenting the 'Dump' to them. It is crucial to ensure that new users are able to quickly grasp and be able to use the 'Dump' so that they can get the most out of this app and see value in using it over its competitors.
  • Consolidation of sections: The 'Lists' and 'Notebooks' sections could be consolidated. Since the ability to create lists within notes was already a planned feature, I could eliminate confusion by removing the lists section -- Users can feel more confident that anything that they would want to do can be found in roughly the same place.
    • Effort was made in further iteration to remove the bottom navigation entirely, to keep the apps main features all in one place.
  • Reduce similarity & number of buttons: There was a need to reduce the number of buttons that look similar. There were lots of buttons with arrows on them, and the app's structure can be reworked to reduce the number of buttons that are necessary in the first place.

Ideating from user feedback

Second iteration (paper wireframes):

  1. (top left) Reduced the number of sections from 4 to 3, with a 'Notebooks', 'Home', and 'Dump' section only.
    • Notebooks section has a full page of all the users notebooks which they can quickly tap into.
    • Opted quickly to abandon this idea because of the reduced access to the 'Dump', and the app seemed to emphasise having a lot of notebooks too much. I predict that users will only end up using 3-5 notebooks on average, so emphasis on notebooks isn't useful.
  2. (top right) Only one page. An idea where I move the emphasis from the notebooks to the 'Dump' by having a drawer for notebooks, and the 'Dump' right at "thumb access" level.
    • Beginning to bring in ideas that components can be reworked into homescreen widgets at the end of the project.
    • I thought that changing the 'active' notebook through a hidden drawer might be unintuitive; it was clear this idea needed work quite quickly.
  3. (bottom left) I develop the idea from [2] by changing the notebook drawer to a quick access bar along the top of the app, above the notes' file navigation system.
    • Seemed to start to look like a good idea, but needed further refinement.
  4. (bottom right) A refinement of [3], and the option I chose to use to develop my low-fidelity prototype.
    • Dump takes up the bottom section of the screen while note file navigation takes up the top part.
    • Ideas floating around at this point that the height of these sections can be adjustable depending on what the user is doing.

4. Low-fidelity prototype

Goals:

This version of the app was built to test:

  • the use of the 'Dump' and how it integrates into the broader app.
  • the text tools within the 'Dump'.
  • user reactions to the overall layout of the app, file navigation, and information structure.

Design explanation:

  • The main screen when you open the app shows (top to bottom) a search bar, a horizontal scroll of the 'notebooks' (folders) that the user has made, the active 'notebook', all of the notes and sub-notes within it (including a note in the 'Travel' notebook that show the ability to create checkbox list items), and finally the 'Dump' at the bottom, which can be contracted to hide (showing more of the file navigation space), or expanded to fullscreen.
  • When the 'Dump' section is expanded to fullscreen, the user can see the text editor screen. The plan is to have this be largely the same across the 'Dump' and with normal notes and sub-notes. The user can add a sample block of text by tapping the keyboard, and use the toolbar above the keyboard to manipulate that active block.
  • To avoid unnecessary over-designing, and to get back to user testing quicker (and partly due to limitations with Figma prototypes), large sections of the app don't do anything.
  • The button with a picture of a house on it that appears in the corner is strictly for the prototype, and served as a quick way to allow the user to start again from the beginning. This was done in response to Figma not supporting the usual swipe-from-the-left-to-go-back function that many phone OSs use. Users were informed about this in the test.

Testing the low-fidelity prototype - Test 2

Goals:

I let 3 people use the prototype alongside a number of tasks (detailed below). I wanted to:

  • see if users understand what the app is and does.
  • compare users expectations of what buttons do with what they actually do.
  • find areas of confusion and frustration to target in subsequent iterations.

Methodology (user test script):

  1. Ask the user background information: what phone do they use, how long per day, and which notes app.
  2. Warn the user that the app is very early in development; many buttons don't work, many graphical elements haven't been developed yet, and that the test is focused on the app's overall functionality.
  3. Warn the user that I will be reading from a script and request that they think out loud as much as possible, especially whenever something seems unintuitive or unexpected.
  4. Ask the user to give me their impressions of the homescreen from top to bottom.
  5. Ask the following questions, skipping wherever the user already answered in response to the previous question:
    1. What is the list in the middle showing? What can you infer from how it's structured?
    2. How would you see notes from the 'Journal' notebook?
    3. What kinds of notebooks/how many would you keep if you were using this app for yourself?
    4. What do you think the 'Dump' is?
    5. How would you add content to the 'Dump'?
    6. Before you do that, what do you think the other buttons around the Dump do?
    7. How do you eel about the 'Dump' right now?
  6. Ask the user to try and make the 'Dump' fullscreen, and then ask:
    1. Where are you now?
    2. What are your impressions of what you see?
    3. Do you see the bar above your keyboard? What do you think it does?
    4. Go ahead and try out some of the buttons on the toolbar. [Warn the user that they will have to undo their changes to apply more].
    5. Would you make use of the 'Dump' if it was a feature in your favourite note-taking app?
    6. On your own phone, how would you go back to the home screen (without using the home button in the corner)?
  7. Finally ask some post-test questions:
    1. What do you think of what you've seen today? Do you have any broad opinions about the app right now?
    2. Supposing that this app was in a finished condition, do you htink that you would use it over your current note-taking method?

Responses and action:

I created a shortlist of goals for the high-fidelity prototype.

  • Fill in placeholders: The prototype should look like a finished product with no placeholder wireframes. This will make the product easier to imagine and users won't be required to fill in the blanks in their mind as much in user tests.
  • Create a dark mode: Dark mode was requested and one user even implied that it would be necessary for the app to be useable for them.
  • Allow users to manually adjust the height of the 'Dump': This will also allow me to consolidate buttons around the 'Dump', which looked similar but did different things.
  • Segregate/distinguish the 'Dump' section: To improve users' understanding of the 'Dump' as quickly as possible, it seemed important to make sure that the 'Dump' was a separate part of the app that interacted with the saved notes, and not child of the selected notebook.
  • Add functionality to menu buttons: Users mentioned that a 'settings' screen was missing, so it was important to add options to the 'Dump', notes, subnotes, and notebooks, so that the app felt more like a finished product.
  • Rework icons that caused confusion: Some of the toolbar icons were confusing to users, or their hit-zone was unclear, and some were just unattractive due to their low fidelity. I planned to remake most of the buttons from scratch.
  • Make the functionality of the 'notification' button more clear: Even though a little icon appeared next to the block to which a notification was applied, one user was confused about what that 'notification' button did. It seemed important to highlight blocks that had had a notification applied clearly and noticeablely, but not in a way that could be distracting.
  • Add search within the text editor: One user requested this and it would be very simple to add at this stage. Users in the next test will be able to see the button and imagine its functionality.

5. High-fidelity prototype

Colours:

  • I learned how to create a colour mode system so that I could implement a light mode at a later date.
  • The prototype uses 3 dark colours, an overlay/text light colour, a colour for links, and a colour for warnings.

Text:

  • I created text styles for a serif and non-serif version of the text in the prototype.
  • I used the fonts IBM Plex Sans and IBM Plex Serif because they can be easily interchanged. These fonts allow the serif toggle in the text editor to work seamlessly.

Components:

  • I created components for all of the atoms and molecules for the design.
  • I started by making small buttons and icons, which served as the "atoms", and then combined them into slightly larger "molecules", such as the box that contains a note's name, options and the ability to add a new sub-note beneath it.
  • I learnt a lot about atomic design before starting this part of the project, and how to use components in Figma to create more efficient and easily-iterable designs.
  • I was surprised to find how much simpler designing and building prototypes was with this approach. I'll certainly be implementing it into my future workflow.
  • The 'Dump' took the most effort to make, because it had to change size when its handle was swiped up or down.
  • The only pre-built assets I used were the fonts, and the iPhone keyboard assets from Apple.

Testing the high-fidelity prototype - Test 3

Goals:

I let 3 more people use the prototype alongside a new set of tasks (detailed below). I wanted to:

  • see if users can identify what the app is and does.
  • see how users react to the presence and functionality of the ‘Dump’.
  • see how users react to the navigation of notes and notebooks, and to the creation of new notes.
  • see how users react to the text editor, the methods of editing note properties (title, icon), and manipulating text with the toolbar.

Methodology (user test script):

  1. Let them know that you will read from a script for consistency across tests.
  2. Welcome to the test! I’ll first ask you to keep in mind that I’m not testing you. Rather, today you’ll be testing a prototype of an app, and we’ll be checking to see how easy the app is to understand and use. By “prototype”, we mean that this is not a real, fully-developed app, but instead a blueprint of how the final product should look and feel. Many of the buttons don’t have any functionality assigned to them, but you will still be able to do everything we hope to, provided that the app is easy enough to use.
  3. Before we get started, I’d like to thank you for participating and let you know that it really helps us to know how to develop the product. In order for us to get the most out of the session today, I’d like to urge you to think out loud as much as possible. Any time something is unintuitive, or isn’t where you expect it to be, or is frustrating, please let me know. Don’t worry about hurting anyone’s feelings – we really do want to hear all your reactions, complaints and all.
  4. Let’s get started. Please open the app and, before you tap anything, let me know your initial reactions to what you think the app broadly is, and what you think it can do.
    1. What sticks out to you?
    2. Is there anything that has an unclear function?
  5. Let’s look at the ‘Dump’ on the lower half of the screen. Let me know your reactions to it if you haven’t already.
  6. Your task now is to find out what the ‘Dump’ is, and what it can do. Remember to think and react out loud, and let me know if anything is frustrating or unintuitive.
  7. Tell the user to finish exploring for now. Could you summarise your reactions to the ‘Dump’?
    1. Could you figure out what it was/did?
    2. Would you use this feature if it was in your favourite note-taking app?
  8. Restart the prototype, contract the Dump. Your next task is to figure out how this navigation system works. Remember to think and react out loud, and let me know if anything is frustrating or unintuitive.
  9. Tell the user to finish exploring for now. What did you learn?
    1. How are notes in this app organised?
    2. Does this system make sense to you?
    3. How does this compare to the note organisation system on your favourite note-taking app?
  10. Your next task is to add some text to a note or to the ‘Dump’. You can do this in the ‘Biscuit Recipe’ note, or in the full-screen ‘Dump’.
  11. How does this text editor view compare to your expectations for a note-taking app?
  12. Finally, please go through the icons on the toolbar, and let me know what you expect each one to do. You can test these buttons too, but bear in mind that, due to the limitations of the prototype, only one button can be active at a time. Please undo your changes after you apply them in order to apply a different change. Again, remember to state your predictions, and to think and react out loud.
  13. That concludes the tasks for this test! Finally, I’ll just ask a few questions about you and your experience today.
    1. What phone do you use?
    2. What is your favourite note-taking app?
    3. Did the term “notebook” make sense to you? How would you explain what a “notebook” is in this app?
    4. Did the term “pipe” make sense to you? How would you explain what “piping [content]” is in this app?
    5. Would you use this app over your favourite note-taking app? Why/Why not? What key features are present/absent?
    6. This app is called “Dumpy”. What are your reactions to that name?
  14. Thank you very much for all of your help today! Have a wonderful day!
